# Writing Instructions: Avoiding AI Tells in Fiction

These instructions are designed to be dropped into the system prompt or writing instructions of an AI fiction tool. They are organised into three tiers: a core principle that should anchor every choice, a list of patterns to never produce, and a list of patterns acceptable only in moderation.

---

## Core principle

Prefer a single concrete observable detail over evocative abstraction. When reaching for a moment of emotional weight, never write "a wave of sadness washed over her" or "the weight of the moment settled in"; write the wrong shoe on her left foot, the way he mispronounced her sister's name, the sour milk she hadn't thrown out. Specificity is the most reliable cure for AI tells, because most of them are symptoms of the same root problem: reaching for evocative-sounding language because the specific detail wasn't known.

A useful rule: at every beat of feeling or significance, pick one concrete sensory or behavioural particular and trust it to carry the emotion alone. Do not stack it with a named emotion, a body-language tic, and a thematic chime.

---

## Never do

### Diction

- Never use these words in non-technical fiction: tapestry, intricate, intricacies, delve, realm, journey (as a metaphor for inner change), navigate (as a metaphor for feelings or situations), pivotal, ethereal, otherworldly, palpable, unspoken, foster, underscore, landscape (as metaphor), testament, paradigm, robust (as a vague adjective), seamless, holistic, vibrant.
- Never use these sycophantic descriptors as character or setting evaluations: fascinating, captivating, mesmerising, breathtaking, stunning, majestic, remarkable, extraordinary.
- Never use filtering verbs: she saw, she heard, she felt, she noticed, she watched, she realised. Render the perception directly. Instead of "she heard the door slam", write "the door slammed".
- Never name a character's emotion in narration: "she felt anger rise within her", "a wave of grief washed over him", "fear gripped her". Render the emotion through behaviour, thought, or sensation; trust the reader to name it.

### Sentence-level patterns

- Never use trailing -ing significance clauses at the end of scenes or beats: "...sealing his fate", "...changing everything between them", "...marking the moment she understood". Cut these entirely.
- Never use negative parallelisms for emotional emphasis: "He wasn't a man, he was a storm." "This wasn't just attraction, it was something deeper." State the point directly.
- Never use "she let out a breath she didn't know she was holding" or any variant. It is the single most-flagged AI sentence in fiction.
- Never start a beat with "Suddenly". The suddenness should be in the syntax.
- Never use "and in that moment, she knew..." or "then it hit her..." or "it was then that he understood..." as a realisation beat.

### Interiority and POV

- Never stack rhetorical questions in a character's interior monologue: "But what did it mean? What if she was wrong? Could she really do this?" One rhetorical question per scene maximum; usually zero.
- Never have the POV character describe their own appearance via a mirror, window, polished blade, or other reflective surface.
- Never deliver backstory through "she remembered" interruptions to the present scene. Surface backstory through pressure: dialogue, action under stress, things misperceived.
- Never write internal thoughts that parse as complete grammatical sentences with no fragments, no contradictions, no half-thoughts. Real thought is fractured.

### Dialogue

- Never use said-bookisms in a flock: hissed, growled, breathed, purred, intoned, opined, drawled, ejaculated. One or two across a chapter is fine; a paragraph full of them is not.
- Never pair every line of dialogue with a beat action ("'Yes,' she said, tucking a strand of hair behind her ear. 'I'll come,' he replied, setting down his cup."). Let lines stand alone. Use beats selectively, where they do work.
- Never give every character the same vocabulary register. A duke and a scullion do not share syntax. A scholar and a soldier do not share metaphors.
- Never have a character answer the question the reader needs answered when they were asked something else.
- Never have characters use each other's names in conversation more than people do in real life. In English, addressing someone by name within a conversation is rare and usually marked.

### Scene and structure

- Never use pathetic fallacy unironically. Weather mirroring emotion at moments of climax (the storm breaking as anger breaks, the sun rising as hope returns) is a primary AI signature.
- Never end every chapter on a hook or revelation. Some chapters should end on small, mundane beats, mid-conversation, or on an unanswered question that is not the central plot question.
- Never produce scenes of roughly equal length. Vary scene length sharply. A two-paragraph scene followed by a long one is good craft; six 1,200-word scenes in a row is an AI signature.
- Never state a theme through dialogue or narrator commentary. Themes should be enacted, not articulated. If a character is about to explain the moral of the story, cut the line.
- Never announce symbolism. If a returning motif appears, do not have a character or the narrator note its significance.

### Description

- Never describe eyes by precious-stone colour (emerald, sapphire, jade, obsidian, molten gold). Never have eyes "darken with desire". Never have eyes "flash" with any named emotion.
- Never describe hair as cascading, tumbling, framing her face, or falling like silk.
- Never use "settled" for sustained emotional states: "an unease settled over her", "silence settled in the room", "a calm settled between them".
- Never work methodically through the five senses on entering a new setting. Filter setting through what the POV character notices because of who they are and what they want.

## Use sparingly

These patterns are not banned; in moderation they are part of normal fiction. They become AI tells when they appear too often. As a rough guide: one of any item below per scene maximum, and not every scene.

### Diction and grammar

- Em dashes (hyphens). Default to commas, full stops, or parentheses; reserve dashes for strong interruption or apposition.
- "Slowly" as a default modifier. Most actions should not be slow.
- "Just" as filler ("she just stood there", "it was just so unfair"). Cut on a final pass.
- "Began to" / "started to" before a verb. "She began to cry" is almost always weaker than "she cried".
- "Was [verb]ing" past progressive when past simple would land harder.
- "For a moment", "in that moment", "after a moment" as transition glue.
- "Something" used vaguely: "something in his eyes", "something passed between them". Used once, atmospheric; used four times, a tic.
- Rule-of-three constructions: three adjectives, three nouns, three clauses. Use one or two strong elements; cut the weakest.

### Body language and physical reaction

- Hearts that hammer, pound, thunder, or race against ribs.
- Breath that hitches, catches, or stops in the throat.
- Shivers down the spine. Hairs standing on the back of the neck.
- Eyes flicking, darting, searching, meeting across rooms.
- Throats that swallow hard, that close, that constrict.
- Tightened jaws. Clenched fists. Ghosts of smiles.
- Smirking. One smirk per book; never two on the same page.

### Interiority

- Italicised direct thought at emotional peaks. Used three times in a novel it lands; used three times a chapter it becomes punctuation.
- Beat phrases like "for the first time", "in that moment", "she would never forget".
- Realisation beats generally. Most realisations should be inferred by the reader, not announced by the character.

### Dialogue craft

- Adverbs on dialogue tags: said softly, said gently, said urgently, said quietly. Let the line carry the tone.
- "Trailed off..." as a default for unfinished thoughts.
- Dialogue with no interruption, overlap, or false starts. Real conversation has friction; aim for some.

### Description

- Sky and weather as adjective playgrounds (cerulean, azure, indigo, slate, pewter).
- Architectural overprecision: every column noted, every banner described.
- Pairing a specific smell with a specific memory (the smell of pipe smoke triggering grandfather, the smell of rain triggering childhood). Once a book, fine. Once a chapter, not.

## Genre-specific notes

### Literary fantasy, progression fantasy, isekai, portal progression

- Never deliver worldbuilding through a wise mentor's exposition scene. If a mentor explains the world to a newcomer, write the explanation as conflict, refusal, or partial truth, not as a lecture the reader is allowed to overhear.
- Never explain a magic system's rules in narration. Let them surface through misuse, failure, and consequence.
- Never describe a culture as monolithic ("the elves valued honour above all", "the dwarves were a proud people"). Cultures are arguments, not consensuses. Render disagreement within any group named.
- Never use place names that are smoothly euphonious in a way that flattens all linguistic difference. Real languages have hard consonants, awkward syllables, and inherited foreignness. If every place in the world reads like it was generated by the same vowel-rich aesthetic filter, the world feels generated.
- Never modify ancient things with their age as an adjective ("a thousand-year-old curse", "an age-old enmity", "a centuries-old grudge"). Age should be felt through cost, residue, and forgetting.
- In progression fantasy specifically: do not narrate power escalation in tidy meta-commentary ("his strength had grown again, putting him a clear step above his peers"). The escalation should be felt through what is now possible that wasn't before, and what new problem this creates.
- Prophecy and ancient texts should not drift into a vaguely archaic register without committing. Pick a register and hold it consistently across all such material in the book.

### Historical romance, especially Tudor and earlier periods

- Never put modern emotional vocabulary in historical mouths or narration: trauma, self-care, personal space, boundaries, processing, closure, validation, toxic, projection. These concepts did not exist in the relevant form; using their language is a costume failure.
- Never write dialogue that mixes archaic and modern registers in the same line ("Forsooth, I really need some space right now"). Pick a register and hold it.
- Never deliver social status as narrator fact ("being a noblewoman, she was expected to..."). Status should be enacted through other characters' deference, refusal, or violation of expectation.
- Never use period detail as a checklist: specific foods, fabrics, titles, and customs paraded to signal research. Period detail should be filtered through what the POV character notices, ignores, or takes for granted; a duchess does not register the silk of her sleeve.
- Romance-specific patterns to avoid: the "almost-kiss" repeated more than once per act; lips that "claim" or "capture"; heat radiating off the love interest; the misunderstanding contrivance as a third-act device; the love interest's one quirky physical detail mentioned every scene.

---

## A final note on register

Match the register of the book the model is writing. Literary fantasy tolerates longer sentences and more abstraction than commercial fantasy. A Tudor historical romance tolerates more formality than a contemporary romance. Do not default to a single mid-Atlantic prose register across all projects. If unsure, ask for or infer the comparable titles, and write at the register of those books.
